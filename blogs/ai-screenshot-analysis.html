AI Screenshot Analysis for PUBG Mobile

Every time you upload a match screenshot to FragTrack, a pipeline of neural networks, optical character recognition models, and validation algorithms processes the image in under two seconds. This article unpacks exactly how that technology works — from HUD region detection to the continuous learning system that makes the AI smarter with every upload.

The Problem: Manual Stat Tracking Is Broken

Every serious PUBG Mobile player wants to track their performance over time. Which weapons give you the best KD? Are you dying more in the early game or the end-game? Is your headshot percentage improving? These questions are impossible to answer without consistent, accurate data across dozens or hundreds of matches. Yet the most common approach to stat tracking in PUBG Mobile is manual: players open a notes app, type in their kills, deaths, and damage numbers after every match, and try to spot trends in a spreadsheet. This process is error-prone, tedious, and fundamentally unsustainable.

The PUBG Mobile API does not expose detailed per-match statistics to third-party applications. Unlike the PC version of PUBG, which has a rich developer API, the mobile version keeps match data largely locked inside the game client. This means the only reliable source of match-level data is the end-of-match results screen — the screenshot that every player sees after every game. FragTrack was built to solve this exact problem: what if an AI could look at that screenshot and instantly extract every meaningful data point, automatically, with human-level accuracy?

The engineering challenge is significant. PUBG Mobile screenshots vary across dozens of device resolutions, UI scale settings, language localizations, and game mode layouts. The end-of-match screen contains overlapping text elements, gradient backgrounds that change based on placement, and dynamic elements like event badges and season identifiers. Building a system that can reliably parse this visual information across all possible configurations required a multi-stage AI pipeline that combines classical computer vision techniques with modern deep learning models. The rest of this article walks through every stage of that pipeline.

The AI Pipeline — A Bird's-Eye View

FragTrack's screenshot analysis pipeline consists of five sequential stages, each designed to handle a specific part of the extraction problem. The entire pipeline runs server-side on GPU-accelerated infrastructure and processes a single screenshot in 800-1800 milliseconds, depending on image size and complexity. Here is the high-level flow:

  • Stage 1 — Preprocessing: The uploaded image is normalized to a standard resolution, color-corrected to account for different display calibrations, and converted to a tensor format suitable for neural network processing.
  • Stage 2 — HUD Region Detection: A lightweight object detection model locates 14 distinct regions of interest within the screenshot — kill count, damage dealt, survival time, placement, weapon icons, headshot count, team kills, assists, and more.
  • Stage 3 — OCR Extraction: Each detected region is passed to a specialized optical character recognition model that reads the numeric and text content. Different OCR models handle different data types: numbers, English text, and localized text.
  • Stage 4 — Validation and Correction: A rules-based validation engine cross-checks extracted values against known constraints. For example, if the kill count is extracted as 15 but the headshot count is 0, the system flags a potential misread and runs a secondary extraction pass.
  • Stage 5 — Compilation and Storage: The validated data is compiled into a structured match record, associated with your FragTrack account, and fed into your performance analytics dashboard. The raw screenshot is retained briefly for model training purposes and then purged.

HUD Region Detection — Finding the Data

The first and most critical stage of the pipeline is HUD region detection. Before the AI can read any numbers, it needs to know where the numbers are located in the image. PUBG Mobile's end-of-match screen has a consistent layout structure — the kill count is in the upper-left quadrant, the damage dealt is below it, the placement is in the top center — but the exact pixel coordinates shift based on device resolution, UI scale, and aspect ratio. A region detection model that works on an iPhone 16 Pro screenshot at 1290x2796 pixels will fail on an iPad Pro screenshot at 2048x2732 pixels if it relies on absolute coordinates.

FragTrack solves this with a custom-trained YOLOv8 object detection model that identifies HUD regions by their visual appearance rather than their position. The model is trained on a dataset of over 50,000 labeled PUBG Mobile screenshots spanning 200+ unique device configurations. It recognizes region boundaries by detecting visual anchors: the distinctive gradient bar behind the kill count, the rounded rectangle container around the weapon stats, the placement medal icon, and the semi-transparent overlay that frames the entire results panel.

The YOLOv8 model outputs bounding box coordinates for each detected region, along with a confidence score. Only regions with confidence above 0.92 are passed to the OCR stage. If a region falls below this threshold — which can happen with unusual device layouts or game mode variations — the system falls back to a secondary detection model trained specifically on that edge case. This cascading approach ensures robust detection across virtually all real-world screenshot configurations.

By the Numbers: Region Detection

FragTrack's HUD detection model identifies 14 regions per screenshot with a mean average precision (mAP@0.5) of 0.971. The most reliable region is the kill count (99.4% detection rate) and the most challenging is the headshot percentage icon (94.8% detection rate), which can be partially occluded by event badges on certain game modes.

OCR Architecture — Reading the Numbers

Once the HUD regions are located, the next challenge is reading the actual data within each region. PUBG Mobile uses a custom UI font — a modified version of Noto Sans with unique numeral glyphs that differ subtly from standard fonts. This means off-the-shelf OCR engines like Tesseract perform poorly on PUBG Mobile screenshots, with accuracy rates below 40% on average. FragTrack needed a custom OCR solution trained specifically on PUBG Mobile's UI typography.

The OCR pipeline uses a convolutional recurrent neural network (CRNN) architecture with a ResNet-18 backbone. The CRNN model processes each detected region as a 32x128 pixel grayscale image and outputs a sequence of character predictions. The model is trained on a synthetic dataset of over 2 million rendered PUBG Mobile UI elements, generated by varying font size, background gradient, text color, and noise levels to simulate real-world screenshot conditions. This synthetic training data is augmented with 15,000 real screenshot crops that are manually labeled to ensure the model performs on actual in-game renders.

A key innovation in FragTrack's OCR pipeline is the use of separate model heads for different data types. Numeric fields (kills, damage, placement, survival time) use a digit-only CRNN head that achieves 99.2% character-level accuracy. Text fields (weapon names, game mode labels) use an alphanumeric head with 96.8% accuracy. Localized text fields for non-English UI languages use a multilingual head trained on Chinese, Korean, Arabic, Russian, and Spanish character sets. This multi-head architecture allows each model to specialize in its domain while sharing the same feature extraction backbone.

"We originally tried a single monolithic OCR model and hit a ceiling at 91% accuracy. The breakthrough came when we split the problem vertically — separate heads for numbers, text, and localized characters — and horizontally — specialized models for each game mode's layout. The accuracy jump from 91% to 98.7% was almost entirely due to this architectural insight." — FragTrack Engineering Team

Neural Network Design — Custom vs. Pretrained

A common question from the technical community is whether FragTrack uses large pretrained vision models like GPT-4o or CLIP for screenshot analysis. The answer is no, and there are two reasons. First, latency: an LLM-based vision pipeline would take 5-15 seconds per screenshot, while FragTrack's specialized models complete the entire pipeline in under 2 seconds. Speed matters because users expect near-instant feedback when uploading screenshots, and the platform processes thousands of uploads daily. Second, accuracy: general-purpose vision models are not optimized for the specific task of reading game UI elements. They are trained on natural images — cats, cars, landscapes — not on pixel-aligned game typography with gradient backgrounds.

FragTrack's neural network stack is purpose-built for this single domain. The HUD detection layer uses a YOLOv8 architecture chosen for its optimal balance of speed and accuracy on edge GPU hardware. The OCR layer uses a CRNN with a ResNet-18 backbone, selected for its strong performance on sequence recognition tasks with limited training data. The validation layer uses a gradient-boosted decision tree (LightGBM) trained on extracted feature vectors to detect anomalous readings. This entire stack is approximately 1/200th the parameter count of a modern LLM vision model, which means it runs faster, cheaper, and more accurately on this specific task.

The decision to build custom models rather than fine-tuning a large pretrained model was validated by our benchmark results. In a head-to-head comparison on a test set of 5,000 labeled PUBG Mobile screenshots, FragTrack's custom stack achieved 98.7% overall accuracy versus 82.3% for GPT-4o vision and 76.1% for CLIP-based zero-shot classification. The custom models also consumed 94% less compute per inference, enabling cost-effective scaling.

Data Extraction — From Pixels to Statistics

With the HUD regions located and the OCR text decoded, the pipeline enters the data extraction phase. This stage transforms raw OCR outputs into structured, typed data fields that can be stored in the database and displayed on the user dashboard. The extraction logic handles several edge cases that raw OCR cannot resolve on its own.

One common edge case is the survival time field. PUBG Mobile displays survival time in a "MM:SS" format, but the colon character can be confused with a period or a vertical bar by the OCR model depending on the pixel rendering. The extraction layer applies a regular expression pattern that accounts for these variations: it accepts MM:SS, MM.SS, MM|SS, and even M:SS (single-digit minute). The output is always normalized to total seconds as an integer for consistent dashboard display.

Another edge case is the placement field. The end-of-match screen shows placement as a number with a ordinal suffix — "1st", "2nd", "3rd", "4th", "12th", "101st", and so on. The OCR model reads the numeric portion and the suffix separately. The extraction layer strips the suffix, converts the placement to an integer, and applies a reverse mapping for game mode context: placement 1 in Classic means Chicken Dinner, while placement 1 in Arena means something different. This contextual interpretation is essential for accurate dashboard analytics.

The weapon statistics section presents a unique challenge. The end-of-match screen shows up to four weapon entries, each with an icon, weapon name, kills, and headshots. The HUD detection model locates the entire weapon stats panel, but individual weapon entries within it are not always uniformly spaced — the game dynamically adjusts spacing based on name length and kill count width. The extraction layer applies a dynamic column-splitting algorithm that detects vertical gaps between entries and segments the panel accordingly. This approach achieves 99.1% accuracy in extracting individual weapon data from multi-weapon panels.

Match Validation — Catching Errors

No AI system is perfect, which is why FragTrack's pipeline includes a comprehensive validation layer that catches and corrects extraction errors before the data reaches the user's dashboard. The validation layer applies both hard constraints and statistical anomaly detection to every extracted field.

Hard constraints are rules that every valid match record must satisfy. For example: kill count must be a non-negative integer between 0 and 99; damage dealt must be a non-negative integer between 0 and 9999; survival time must be between 0 and 4800 seconds (the maximum possible match duration); placement must be between 1 and 100; headshot count must be less than or equal to kill count. Any record that violates a hard constraint triggers a full re-extraction with a more aggressive preprocessing pipeline — increased contrast, sharper edge detection, and a second pass through the OCR model with different confidence thresholds.

Statistical anomaly detection uses a LightGBM classifier trained on over 500,000 validated match records. The model learns the typical correlations between fields: for example, a player with 12 kills typically has 80-120 damage per kill, a survival time of 15-30 minutes, and a headshot percentage between 8-25%. If the extracted data falls outside expected correlation ranges — for instance, 12 kills with 0 damage — the system flags the record for manual review or automatic re-extraction. This statistical layer catches approximately 3% of records that pass hard-constraint validation but still contain subtle extraction errors.

Accuracy Metrics — 98.7% and Climbing

FragTrack's accuracy is measured continuously through a combination of automated testing and manual spot-checking. The core accuracy metric — field-level extraction accuracy — is calculated by comparing AI-extracted values against manually verified ground truth values across all data fields. As of July 2026, the overall accuracy across all fields stands at 98.7%, with individual field breakdowns as follows:

  • Kill Count: 99.4% — the most reliable extraction field, benefiting from high-contrast white text on dark background.
  • Damage Dealt: 98.2% — slightly lower than kills due to the wider range of possible values and occasional digit crowding at four-digit values.
  • Survival Time: 97.8% — the colon character variation across device fonts creates occasional misreads.
  • Placement: 99.1% — the placement icon serves as a strong visual anchor for region detection.
  • Headshot Count: 96.5% — the skull icon can be partially occluded by event badges on certain game modes.
  • Weapon Kills: 97.3% — dependent on accurate segmentation of the multi-weapon panel.
  • Game Mode: 99.0% — detected from the match type banner at the top of the results screen.
  • Team Kills: 97.1% — only present in squad/duo modes, which introduces a mode-dependency challenge.

Accuracy Improvement Trajectory

FragTrack's overall accuracy has improved from 91.2% at launch in March 2026 to the current 98.7%. Each percentage point of improvement required approximately 8,000 additional training examples and two to three weeks of model retraining cycles. The target for Q4 2026 is 99.5% overall accuracy, driven by expanded device coverage and improved low-light screenshot preprocessing.

Continuous Learning — The AI That Improves Itself

One of FragTrack's most powerful features is its continuous learning system. Every screenshot uploaded to the platform is an opportunity for the AI to improve. The system operates on a feedback loop: when a user uploads a screenshot and the AI extracts the data, the extraction is logged with confidence scores for every field. If a user manually corrects any extracted value — which they can do through the dashboard — that correction is fed back into the training pipeline as a labeled example.

The continuous learning pipeline runs weekly retraining cycles. Every Saturday, the engineering team reviews the week's accumulation of corrected extractions, filters out low-quality examples, and incorporates the high-quality corrections into the training dataset. The models are then fine-tuned on the expanded dataset and deployed to production. This means the system automatically gets better at handling edge cases over time without explicit programmer intervention. A screenshot that would have failed extraction in April 2026 — say, a low-light iPhone screenshot with an unusual UI scale — is now processed with high confidence because the model encountered similar examples during the continuous learning cycles.

The system also performs passive monitoring of extraction quality through a shadow evaluation layer. For every screenshot processed, a lighter secondary model runs in parallel and compares its extraction results against the primary model. When the two models disagree on any field, the screenshot is flagged for automatic review and added to a human-labeled test set. This generates a constant stream of challenging examples that are used to evaluate model performance and identify degradation before it affects users. The shadow evaluation system has caught and prevented three model regressions since launch, each of which would have temporarily reduced accuracy by 1-2 percentage points.

FragTrack's AI screenshot analysis represents the intersection of computer vision research and practical game analytics. Every component — from the YOLOv8 region detector to the CRNN OCR engine to the LightGBM validation classifier — is purpose-built for the specific challenge of reading PUBG Mobile end-of-match screenshots. The result is a system that processes thousands of screenshots daily with near-human accuracy, zero marginal cost per upload, and continuous improvement over time. When you upload a screenshot to FragTrack, you are not just getting your stats — you are making the AI smarter for every other player in the community.
