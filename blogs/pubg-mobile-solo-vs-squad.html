Solo vs Squad: Adjusting Your PUBG Mobile Playstyle

The same aggressive push that wins a squad fight will get you killed in solos. Here's how your looting, positioning, and fight decisions should actually change depending on who's with you.

Table of Contents

  2. Solo Looting & Positioning
  3. Solo Fight Selection
  4. Squad Roles — Who Does What
  5. Squad Fight Selection
  6. Switching Between the Two

The Core Difference

Solo and squad aren't just the same game with a different headcount — they reward almost opposite instincts. In squads, aggression is often correct because you have backup, revives, and combined firepower. In solos, that same aggression usually just gets you killed faster, because there's no one to third-party the third-partier, and no one to revive you if it goes wrong.

Players who only play one mode often carry the wrong instincts into the other — solo players who play too passively in squads, and squad players who push too hard when they queue solo. Recognizing which mode you're in, and adjusting accordingly, is a bigger skill jump than most people give it credit for.

Solo Looting & Positioning

In solos, every fight is a fight you have to win alone, so positioning matters more than loot volume. Prioritize a full loadout over a perfect one — a decent AR with basic attachments beats an unfinished perfect kit if it means you're combat-ready ten minutes earlier. Avoid overextending into hot-drop compounds early; in squads, four guns clearing a building is fast, but solo, you're checking every angle by yourself while someone else might already be watching the door.

Solo Fight Selection

The single biggest solo skill is knowing which fights to avoid entirely. Third-partying is far more dangerous solo, since there's no teammate to watch your back while you focus the first threat. A good rule: if you can hear or see a fight already happening, let it play out and clean up the survivor rather than diving into a three-way with full health opponents.

Disengaging is also just more viable solo than most players use it. There's no teammate depending on you to hold a position — if a fight is going badly, repositioning or retreating is almost always better than dying on principle.

Squad Roles — Who Does What

Squads work best when players actually commit to a role instead of everyone trying to do everything. A rough breakdown that shows up in most competitive squads: an IGL calling rotations and fight decisions, one or two fraggers pushing and initiating engagements, and a support player managing utility, reviving, and covering angles. FragTrack's Players and Team pages are built around exactly this — tracking who's playing which role and how the roster performs together, not just individually.

Squad Fight Selection

Squads can take fights solos should avoid — third-partying is genuinely strong here, since a fresh, coordinated squad diving on two exhausted, low-health teams is usually a favorable trade. Communication is what separates squads that win these fights from squads that just get caught in the crossfire — calling enemy positions, health status, and rotation plans in real time matters more than individual aim once you're past the early game.

Switching Between the Two

If you play both modes regularly, the fastest improvement usually comes from consciously naming which mindset you're in before the match starts, rather than letting habits bleed across. Ask yourself: can I actually afford this fight alone, or am I subconsciously expecting backup that isn't coming? That one question catches a surprising number of avoidable solo deaths.

Whichever mode you play more, the stats that matter shift slightly — solo players should watch their 1v1 engagement win rate closely, while squad players get more value from tracking team-wide trends.
